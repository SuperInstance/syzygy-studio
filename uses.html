Uses: one camera, one job

A camera exists for a purpose, so the teaching decomposes by purpose: a model never learns "vision" — it learns the specific visual work of its specific camera. Narrowness is the feature. It's what makes the model tiny, and the tiny model is what fits on cheap hardware everywhere.

The ladder runs: browser → an Uno Q-class board → a Jetson-class module → the training machine with the big teacher model. Each exhibit below names its camera, its job, what it must never miss, and why narrow wins there.

1. The harbor inventory walker

The camera: walking height, on a small robot doing rounds of a working harbor — docks, finger piers, fuel shed, tide line.

The job: inventory as it walks — which boats are in which slips, which crates are where — plus the 3D line work of staying on the dock: know the edges, yield to people, and pull into a finger pier to let a cart pass.

Must never miss: the edge of the dock, and a person stepping out from behind a hull.

Why narrow wins: it needs the geometry of this harbor, not a general theory of objects. The docks video on the front page is literally its training data: first-person, moving camera, near pilings sharp and far masts slow — depth you can feel from the rate the letters change.

2. The warehouse forklift

The camera: one view, forward and down, mounted low on an automatic forklift.

The job: two things — object avoidance, and getting the forks into the pallet. That's the whole job description.

Must never miss: a foot in the aisle, and the pallet pocket's exact position at the moment of approach.

Why narrow wins: one view, two jobs, extremely streamlined. A model that does only this can be precise where it matters and small enough for the smallest board on the ladder — and it never pays, in frames or milliseconds, for eyes that watch the ceiling.

3. The packing grabber

The camera: fixed above one station on one conveyor line.

The job: grab that specific item off the belt — its shape, its orientation, its timing — and fold and build that exact box to receive it.

Must never miss: an item arriving skewed, and a human hand entering the frame.

Why narrow wins: hyper-specific is the point. The narrower the world, the more exactly a tiny model can master it — this camera's universe is a metre of belt, and perfection inside a metre is achievable.

4. The overseer

The camera: overhead cameras, plus every robot's own first-person text frames, joined into one top-down picture — the RTS view of the whole floor or harbor.

The job: watch the pattern, not the objects: congestion forming, a walker and a cart converging on the same crossing, a machine stopped where machines don't stop.

Must never miss: two machines arriving at the same crossing at the same time.

Why narrow wins: the overseer reads grids of grids. Each camera below already did its one job and published letters; joining letters is cheap — text frames merge in any order, duplicates and all, with no coordinator. The fleet watches each other; the overseer watches the watching.

Every deployment defines the same three things — this camera, this job, these questions — and the teaching is built from footage of that job. If the use case isn't narrow enough to state in one sentence, it isn't decomposed yet.
