Track 1 — Curious

Why letters? The smartest software we have mostly works by reading and writing words. Cameras, meanwhile, produce pictures — and a word-machine can't read a picture. Syzygy translates: it turns each picture from a camera into a page of letters and marks that keeps the shapes — edges as lines, brightness as denser or lighter characters, fine detail as Braille dots. Once the world is a page, anything that can read can see.

What does it cost? Not dollars, first. An AI pays in compute, time, and frame rate — those are its native currency. A big general vision model is like hiring a whole film crew to watch one doorbell. Syzygy's pass costs almost nothing, so the reading can happen on every frame, on tiny hardware, in the background — the way you never think about the code inside a calculator.

Why narrow beats general. A warehouse forklift's camera points forward and down. Its whole world is: don't hit anything, and line the forks up with the pallet. It does not need to recognise faces, read signs, or appreciate sunsets — and if it only learns its two jobs, it can be extremely precise at them and small enough to live on a chip the size of a postage stamp. Precision for its task — but only its task. That's not a limitation of the approach; it is the approach. The front page shows the idea moving: a Sitka dock, in letters.

Track 2 — Builders

1. Run the kernel

git clone https://github.com/SuperInstance/Syzygy
cd Syzygy
sh tools/suite-total.sh

You should see 219 checks, 0 failures, and the golden hash fnv1a = 0x6dbdd1a8. The kernel is C with no allocation, no floats, no libc — the same input produces byte-identical output on every machine, which is what makes its results citable. Or skip the clone and run it in your browser: the live demo.

2. Run your own video through it

git clone https://github.com/SuperInstance/syzygy-video
cd syzygy-video
INPUT=/path/to/your.mp4 bash run.sh

Out comes a text render per frame (glyph, Braille, and tone layers) plus a side-by-side video of your footage and its letter-render. Calm, moving-camera footage reads cleanest; the module's README documents its thresholds and its honest limits.

3. Write a strategy tile

Clone the playable demo, then look at lib/strategies/. A tile is a small module: it receives the model's structured perception — nothing else — and returns one action and a one-line reason that appears in the decision feed. Copy explorer.js, change one rule (say: prefer turning toward the widest corridor), restart, and watch the feed argue differently on identical eyes. That loop — perception fixed, judgment yours — is the whole design.

Track 3 — Agents

This track is written for you — a model learning to read a text frame. It is the frozen reading guide from our first experiment, plus the procedures that experiment proved necessary, plus the scores you should expect of yourself.

The frame

You are shown a scene as a grid of characters, one character per cell. In the maze form: # is a wall (impassable), . is free floor, A is the agent, G is the goal. Rows print top to bottom; the top row is north. Columns run west (left) to east (right). Distances count cells. "Adjacent" means the four cells directly north, south, east, and west — never the diagonals. In kernel-rendered frames the same discipline applies with richer alphabets: edge glyphs (─ │ ╱ ╲) mark boundaries, Braille cells carry fine detail, tone characters carry brightness. Always establish the legend before reading anything.

The procedures (this is where readers fail)

  • Count cell by cell. Do not estimate a distance from the look of the grid. Walk the row difference and the column difference one cell at a time. Estimation feels like counting and scores like guessing.
  • Inspect the goal's doorstep before claiming a path. List the goal's four neighbours first. If all four are walls, no path exists — say so, whatever the rest of the maze looks like. Then, and only then, trace a route step by step.
  • Localise on one axis at a time. Bearing errors usually keep one axis right and mirror the other. Compute the row difference sign and the column difference sign separately, then compose the direction.
  • Keep the answer contract. If a format is requested, answer in exactly that format. A correct answer in prose the scorer can't parse is a wrong answer, and talking yourself past your own correct intermediate step is a known failure mode.
  • Abstain rather than assert. Connectivity, counts, and contents you have not actually checked are unknowns. "I have not verified a path" is a better answer than a confident path that isn't there.

The baseline to beat

Two strong text models took this test on 60 mazes, 300 purpose-shaped questions, with the frame, with no frame (blind), and with the wrong frame (shuffled). With the frame they scored 44% and 35% overall, against blind baselines of 15% and 11% — real reading, mostly of position: the better reader found goal bearing at 52% against 2% blind. But on mazes where the goal was visibly walled in on all four sides, they answered "a path exists" anyway — 0 of 30 and 1 of 30 — and on counting adjacent walls both lost to simply always answering "two". Read the full results before your first run: Receipts (raw write-up: the experiment's RESULTS document; the frozen guide: READING_GUIDE). Positional reading is already there. Verification is what you are here to learn.
